Count the rooms a city gives you. An office you might enter. A library you might join. A hospital you hope to avoid. Then count the one you use every day without thinking about it, and which nobody signs their name to: the street.

It has no architect, in the sense that no single person is credited with it. It has dozens of authors — a highways engineer, a utilities contractor, a licensing officer, a bus operator, a lighting consultant — none of whom is responsible for the result.

## The budget tells you what a city believes

A mid-sized European city will spend more on one cultural building than on the resurfacing programme for its entire central district over a decade. The cultural building will be photographed. The resurfacing will be experienced by everyone in the city, several times a day, for thirty years.

This is not an argument against cultural buildings. It is an argument about where the design attention goes. The street is the largest continuous piece of public interior any city owns, and it is procured as maintenance.

Maintenance procurement has a specific consequence: the specification is written to be replaceable rather than good. Kerbs are chosen for availability. Paving units are chosen because the contractor already owns the machine that lays them. The result is not ugly by intent. It is ugly by supply chain.

## Four decisions that do most of the work

Street design gets discussed in slogans, which is a shame, because the variables that actually change how a street feels are few and unglamorous:

1. **Kerb height and radius.** A tight corner radius slows every turning vehicle without a sign, a camera, or a fine. A generous one invites speed and then requires enforcement to correct it.
2. **Where the water goes.** Drainage sets the cross-fall, the cross-fall sets where you can put a table, a tree, or a ramp. Most awkward level changes in a public realm scheme are a drainage decision that arrived late.
3. **Tree pit volume.** A tree in two cubic metres of soil is a fifteen-year tree. The same species in fifteen cubic metres is a hundred-year tree. The visible cost is the same; the invisible cost is a bigger hole.
4. **What happens at 3am.** Lighting levels and sightlines determine whether a street is a route or a barrier for half the population. This is measurable and it is routinely not measured.

None of these needs a masterplan. All of them need someone with authority to hold a specification against pressure.

## The utilities problem nobody solves

Under most historic streets is a century of undocumented services. Every scheme that promises high-quality paving eventually meets a gas main whose position is a rumour.

The honest responses are limited. You can survey properly and pay for it, which front-loads cost into a phase that has no visible output. You can lay a shared services duct, which is expensive once and cheap forever, and which almost no authority can fund because the saving lands in someone else's budget in fifteen years. Or you can accept that the surface will be opened every eighteen months and design a paving pattern that tolerates patching — which is what most cities do, without admitting it is a decision.

The third option is not shameful. It just deserves to be chosen rather than defaulted into, because a paving pattern designed for patching looks very different from one designed for permanence.

## What a good street actually gives you

Ask people what they like about a street they enjoy and the answers are consistent and slightly boring. There is somewhere to sit that is not a café. There is shade. The pavement is wide enough that a pram and two people going the other way is not an event. There are more than three kinds of thing happening — a shop, a doorway, a bench, a tree — within any thirty-metre stretch.

That last one is the closest thing urbanism has to a rule. Variety at short intervals is what makes walking feel shorter than it is. It is also the first thing lost when a single owner develops a whole block frontage, which is why so many new districts are pleasant in plan and tedious at eye level.

The street is the only room everyone uses. It ought to be designed like one.
